Starting from the start

What is "Reinforcement Learning"

Reinforcement learning is trial-and-error at scale: an agent (the robot) looks at its current state (what it can see, its battery level), takes an action (move, or stay put), and receives a reward — a number telling it whether that was a good idea or a bad one. Often the effect of an action may yield a reward after a long time. The agent's job is to learn a policy — a rule for picking actions — that maximizes reward summed over an entire episode, not just the next step.

Agent observes state → picks an action → environment responds → reward + new state → repeat

This project uses one specific family of RL algorithm: Q-learning, which doesn't learn a policy directly but instead learns to estimate a Q-value — the expected future reward of taking a given action in a given state, then acting optimally afterward. Once you have good Q-value estimates, the policy is trivial: always take the action with the highest one except when exploring, the agent picks a random action. Deep Q-learning (DQN) is just that idea with a neural network trained to estimate Q-value for a combination of state,action. The state space here with moving pedestrians and a draining battery — is far too large to ever tabulate by hand and the neural network fits in well to fill the gap. Everything below is about what it actually takes to make that work in practice.

The important part of any RL model is the reward signal and how it is being used to update the policy. Exploration, exploitation, Policy and target network, replay buffer, etc are some important concepts that get covered in the blog.

The Environment Setup

What can it sense and feel?

The robot sees a small 10 X 10 window around itself — not the whole map — and has to reach a goal cell that moves every episode, without draining its battery or colliding with anything along the way. Before every move the robot sees the state of the environment along with the reward signal on its previous move. It then consults its learned policy and picks the next action. This loop repeats.

Obstacles are fixed; pedestrians wander with momentum. The battery drains with every move, and running it to zero ends the episode early — so efficiency isn't optional, it's structural. The model is trained in 4 difficulty scenarios where grid size, obstacle density, pedestrian density and step budget all scale together.

Four difficulties — and "easier environment" doesn't mean "learns faster"

The same network architecture and training recipe was pointed at four grid worlds of increasing density — more obstacles, more pedestrians, a bigger map, a longer episode. Below is the trained agent, live, on each.

For each difficulty level a model was trained from scratch. The model at the 150th learning episode has been captured above.

The intuitive guess is that the easiest map should also be the one the agent learns fastest on. The data says the opposite: Easy is actually the slowest scenario to get off the ground, while Medium, Hard, and Expert all reach strong success rates at least as fast. The likely reason is that "easier" and "more informative" aren't the same thing. A near-empty map gives the agent very little to learn from early on — its local view is mostly blank, and it rarely collides with anything, so there's little negative signal teaching it what to avoid. A denser map puts obstacles and pedestrians in view more often and triggers collisions more frequently while the policy is still close to random, which is a noisier but denser stream of feedback about what not to do. More signal, sooner, even if each individual episode is harder to fully solve. (This project never isolated scenario density as its own controlled variable, so this is a plausible reading of the pattern rather than a proven causal claim.)

Replay Buffer and Why?

The replay buffer is a fixed-size, first-in-first-out store of past transitions — (state, action, reward, next state, done) tuples — that the agent samples from at random rather than training directly on the live stream of experience as it happens. Two reasons this matters.

  • Decorrelation: consecutive steps in an episode are highly similar (the robot a moment later is in nearly the same spot, facing nearly the same obstacles), so training on them in order feeds the network a stream of near-duplicate, correlated examples — sampling randomly from a large buffer breaks that correlation, closer to the i.i.d. assumption most gradient-based learning relies on.
  • Reuse: without a buffer, each transition would be used for exactly one gradient update and then discarded; with a replay buffer, a single transition can be resampled and learned from many times before it eventually ages out, which matters a lot when transitions (especially the rare, informative ones — a collision, a delivery) are expensive to collect.

Q-Learning Double Q-Learning

Q-learning, at its core, doesn't try to learn a policy directly — it tries to learn a Q-value: the expected total future reward of taking a given action in a given state, then acting optimally from there on. In any state, just take whichever action has the highest Q-value. The classic version keeps these estimates in a table, one row per state-action pair, updated a little at a time toward each new experience.

A table stops being an option the moment the state space gets large — there's no way to enumerate a row for every possible grid configuration this robot could encounter. DQN (Deep Q-Network) replaces the table with a neural network.

Training that network means giving it something to regress toward, and that's where a second network enters: a policy network that's updated every step, and a target network — a periodically-synced, temporarily-frozen copy — that supplies a stable target for the policy network to chase. Exactly how those two networks divide the work of picking and scoring the next action is the one-line difference between plain DQN and the Double DQN used here, and it's worth seeing precisely why that difference matters. The section below walks through it with the actual equations.

A closer look

From Q-learning to Double DQN, in three equations

Three successive fixes, each one patching a specific failure mode in the one before it. The notation: $\theta$ is the online/policy network's weights, $\theta^-$ is the target network's weights (a lagged copy of $\theta$).

1. Standard (online) Q-learning

Watkins & Dayan, 1992 — tabular Q-learning, and its naive deep-network extension

The classic update rule nudges $Q(s,a)$ toward the observed reward plus the discounted value of the best next action:

Tabular update
$$Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\Big]$$

Replace the table with a neural network $Q(s,a;\theta)$ and the same idea becomes a regression problem: minimize the squared difference between $Q(s,a;\theta)$ and a bootstrapped target built from that same network:

Naive deep Q-learning loss
$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a';\theta)}_{\text{target } y} - Q(s,a;\theta)\big)^2\Big]$$

The moving-target problem. $y$ is computed with the exact same weights $\theta$ that the gradient step is about to change. Every update shifts the thing being regressed toward, which shifts $y$ again on the next step — the optimization target is non-stationary by construction. Combined with correlated, non-i.i.d. samples from a single trajectory, this is prone to oscillation or outright divergence rather than clean convergence.

2. Nature DQN

Mnih et al., 2015 — "Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning," Nature

The fix: freeze a second copy of the network, $\theta^-$, and use it to compute the target, syncing $\theta^- \leftarrow \theta$ only every $C$ steps (this project's target_update_freq):

Fixed Q-targets
$$y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a';\theta^-)$$

Because $\theta^-$ doesn't move between syncs, $y$ is stable for long enough that gradient descent on $\theta$ can actually make progress against it — this is what resolves the moving-target problem above, and it's the target network already described earlier in this post.

But it introduces a new bias: overestimation. Look closely at $\max_{a'} Q(s',a';\theta^-)$: the same frozen network both picks which next action looks best (the $\arg\max$ hiding inside that $\max$) and reports how good that action is. $Q(s',\cdot\,;\theta^-)$ is a noisy estimate — some actions' values are overestimated by approximation error, some underestimated. Taking a $\max$ over noisy estimates is a biased estimator of the true maximum ($\mathbb{E}[\max_a \hat Q(s,a)] \ge \max_a \mathbb{E}[\hat Q(s,a)]$, by Jensen's inequality): the operation preferentially selects whichever action currently looks inflated, and then trusts that same inflated number as the value to bootstrap from. Through repeated bootstrapping, that optimism compounds over training rather than averaging out.

3. Double DQN

van Hasselt, Guez & Silver, 2016 — "Deep Reinforcement Learning with Double Q-learning," AAAI (building on van Hasselt, 2010)

The fix is a one-line change: keep two networks, but split the two jobs the $\max$ was quietly doing in one line into two separate steps, using a different network for each:

Decoupled selection / evaluation
$$y = r + \gamma\, Q\big(s',\ \underbrace{\arg\max_{a'} Q(s',a';\theta)}_{\text{select with policy net}}\ ;\ \theta^-\big)$$

The policy network ($\theta$) picks which action it currently thinks is best. The target network ($\theta^-$) — a different, lagged set of weights, trained on different recent experience — independently scores how good that specific action actually is. This is exactly what's implemented in agent.py's replay().

Why selecting and evaluating with different networks cancels the bias instead of compounding it. The overestimation in Nature DQN happens because one network's own noise is both the reason an action gets picked and the number used to trust it — selection error and evaluation error are perfectly correlated, since they're the same number read twice. Once selection and evaluation come from two different, only weakly-correlated networks, that correlation breaks: $\theta$ might overvalue some action and select it, but $\theta^-$ has its own independent noise and has no particular reason to agree — it might score that same action lower, or another action's error might go the other way on a different transition. Averaged over many updates, these independent errors push in both directions roughly as often as not, so they average out instead of all pointing the same way. It's the same logic as the original tabular Double Q-learning result: two independently-noisy estimators, one nominating and one confirming, don't inherit each other's optimism — a biased single estimator used twice does.

What went wrong first

The Robot couldn't tell Which way the Goal was - The Case of Not Knowing enough

An early training run with lesser observable environmental features just… didn't learn. Reward stayed flat, success rate stayed near zero, no matter how long it trained. Without the dr, dc: normalised (row, column) displacement vector from goal position, the robot doesn't know where to go, unless the goal enters its local view.

Without this sense of direction of the goal, two completely different episodes could produce an identical observation while demanding opposite actions, depending on which side of the robot the goal happened to be on. No amount of extra training fixes that: the observation itself didn't carry enough information to act correctly. Adding a normalized direction vector to the global features resolved it immediately. The Model can learn to observe and respond to this added - useful - state information.

This became the first data point in a broader lesson that shows up again and again below: a model that isn't learning is often not a model problem at all — it's an information problem.
What actually mattered

Seventeen hyperparameters, ranked by how much they matter

Every exposed hyperparameter — from learning rate to network depth to how erratically pedestrians move — was swept independently, three seeds each, to separate a genuine effect from training noise. Ranking all of them by effect size rather than by which tier they happened to be tested in produced a clean, if slightly surprising, picture.

A small handful of choices are existential — remove them and the task collapses. Everything else is comparatively cosmetic, moving performance at the margins without ever threatening whether the robot learns at all.

The reward-shaping result is the more interesting of the two headline effects. reward_progress_scale weights a dense, per-step term added to the reward every single step, not just at delivery:

Progress-shaping term
$$\text{reward} \mathrel{+}= \texttt{reward_progress_scale} \times (\text{prev_distance} - \text{new_distance})$$

Positive when the robot's Manhattan distance to the goal shrank that step, negative when it grew, zero on a STAY — at the default scale of 1.0, moving one cell closer is worth +1. The ablation finding isn't about magnitude at all: any non-zero scale (0.5, 1.0, 2.0) works about equally well, and the only thing that collapses the task is removing the term entirely (scale = 0). The signal has to exist; its exact size barely matters. Meanwhile a long tail of hyperparameters — network width and depth, batch size, battery tightness, view size — turned out to be robust across the whole range tested, which is itself a useful, if less dramatic, finding: it says where tuning effort is not worth spending.

Searching smarter

What a joint hyperparameter search found — and broke

Independent ablation studies the effect of each parameter individually on some objective or multi-objective function. Simply pooling the best hyperparameter values obtained from the ablation study will not give us the best overall hyperparameter combinations since we would be basing our choice on the assumption on the independence of hyperparameters. This demands a search over the hyper parameter space. We shortlist the following 9 hyperparameters observed to be consequential in the ablation study.

Tier 1 — Core DQN

  • learning_rate
  • gamma (γ)
  • epsilon_decay (ε-decay)
  • batch_size
  • target_update_freq
  • replay_buffer_size

Tier 3 — Reward shaping

  • reward_progress_scale
  • reward_delivery
  • collision_penalty (composite — sets both the obstacle and pedestrian collision rewards together)

Core DQN and reward shaping are the two groups most plausibly coupled — reward magnitude sets the scale of the error signal the network trains on, so it directly interacts with how big a learning-rate step or how stale a target network can be. Network architecture and environment-capacity hyperparameters (Tiers 4–5) were left out of the joint search for the same reason: nothing in the ablation results suggested they interact with reward scale the way these two tiers do.

The search's objective was deliberately simple: maximize final success rate, with a tiny bonus for higher episode reward used only to break ties between otherwise-equal trials. Each of the 100 trials trained a fresh agent from scratch on one sampled combination of the 9 hyperparameters above and reported back how well it did — that single number is all the search ever optimized for.

On that objective, the search worked: the top-ranked trials were retrained for real (3 seeds, full 300-episode budget, not the shortened single-seed budget used during search) to confirm the picks weren't just lucky noise, and every one of them converged to a high success rate dramatically faster than anything an independent, per-tier sweep had found — real evidence that core-DQN and reward-shaping hyperparameters do interact.

But the same confirmation run exposed what the objective never measured: reward stability. Every one of the top-ranked candidates came back 3–8× noisier (in reward signal), episode to episode, than the plain defaults — because "maximize success rate" has nothing to say about how consistently that success is achieved. A model that looks fine on average while its outcomes swing wildly per episode isn't actually reliable, and that's exactly the failure mode a single success-rate number hides: the objective had no way to tell a smooth 95%-success policy apart from an erratic one that also happens to land on 95%.

The fix wasn't a bigger search — it was a better question. Re-running the same search as a genuine multi-objective problem (success rate and reward stability, scored and optimized separately rather than collapsed into one number) surfaced a configuration that matched the original search's best speed and success rate while being over an order of magnitude more stable.
Does it transfer?

Training on the easy map vs. training on the hardest one

Every model above was trained from scratch on one scenario. So we ask: does a policy trained on one difficulty hold up on the other three it's never seen? We investigate on this matter with two checkpoint models — one trained only on Easy, one trained only on Expert — were each evaluated cold on all four scenarios, 100 episodes each, zero exploration.

The easy-trained policy is the better agent on its own map, but it's brittle everywhere else: success rate falls off steadily with every step up in density, and collisions climb sharply — from essentially zero on Easy to over 14 per episode on Hard and Expert. It learned a policy that works well in a nearly empty world and has no fallback once the world fills up with things to run into.

The expert-trained policy tells the opposite story. Its success rate is lower on its own training map than the easy-trained policy is on its training map, but that success rate barely moves across all four scenarios — it's not even highest on Expert, the map it was actually trained on. More strikingly, its collision rate stays low and comparatively flat everywhere, including on maps it never trained on. Having spent its entire training budget surrounded by obstacles and pedestrians, it strongly learnt the one skill that matters : not running into things alongside the ultimate goal of moving towards the goal. That's the same mechanism proposed earlier for why denser scenarios learn faster — here it shows up again as why they also generalize better.

Where this leaves things

Conclusion and Summary

The end state is a single-robot policy that handles four densities of the same delivery task without any per-map retuning — but the more interesting output of this project is the trail of evidence behind it.

  • A training run that looked completely broken turned out to be an information problem, not a model problem — the observation didn't contain enough to act on, and no amount of extra training could have fixed that.
  • Across all seventeen exposed hyperparameters, each hyperparameter had a different effect size — three mattered dramatically, and the rest had lesser effect or were completely negligible
  • Two hyperparameter searches told a story about optimizing for the right objective — the first found real speed gains and real instability it never measured; the second existed specifically to go back and measure what the first one missed.
  • Harder scenarios learned faster, not slower — a near-empty map gives an agent very little to learn from, while a denser one hands it a steady stream of collisions to learn to avoid.
  • That same density advantage carries over to generalization — a policy trained only on the hardest map held up across all four difficulties, while a policy trained only on the easiest one excelled at home and fell apart everywhere else.
